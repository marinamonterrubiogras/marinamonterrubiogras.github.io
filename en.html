The product page

Product page

Every block on the product page has a goal: showing the product well, helping the customer choose the right size and helping her discover more products. I adapt it to each product by type and sizing: a one-size accessory is not the same as a sized garment.

1 2 3 4 5 6 7
  1. 1
    Gallery

    I decide the order of the photos: flat lay, e-commerce and campaign. The flat lay shows the product, its fabric and its shape; the e-commerce shots show how it fits the model, and the campaign images elevate the brand.

  2. 2
    Colour variants

    They show the other colours of the same product visually, so the customer can choose without leaving the page.

  3. 3
    Size guide

    I build the guide with the measurements from Design. It helps the customer choose her size and so reduces exchanges and returns.

  4. 4
    Description

    The product name, colour and material appear naturally in the text, because that is what the customer searches for. This also improves SEO.

  5. 5
    Complete your look

    Items that go with the product and appear in the photos. It adds value to the page and drives cross-selling, which helps raise the average order value.

  6. 6
    Recommended products

    More cross-selling at the bottom of the page. I rotate them between best sellers, new arrivals and products that need more visibility.

  7. 7
    Labels

    I manage product labels such as Best seller or Personalisable. They appear on product cards across the site: New In, collections and recommendations.

What I look at

Signals I review

Clarity · behaviour
  • Dead clicks: clicks on elements that aren’t links.
  • Rage clicks: repeated clicks on one spot, a sign of frustration.
  • Quick backs: a sign they didn’t find what they expected.
  • Scroll depth: how far people scroll on the homepage and the grid.
  • Mobile vs desktop: differences in behaviour by device.

CRM · Waitlist

Coming soon

When an item doesn’t arrive in time for its drop, or sells out and a restock is planned, it stays live on the site as coming soon. The wait turns into demand.

1The product is late or sells out

The item isn’t in stock in time for the drop, or sells out and a restock is planned.

2It goes live as coming soon

It appears on the site with the option to join the waitlist. This builds desire and a queue to buy it, and it can become a highly demanded product.

3It arrives and goes into stock

On the day it arrives, stock is activated on the site.

4The flow sends the alert

Klaviyo alerts the whole waitlist, which makes it a flow with good conversion.

Strategy

Drop launches

Every drop has its own cycle. Depending on the number of items, I adjust the timings and build the calendar around them. As an example, this is how a four-week cycle is organised.

Week 1

Planning

  • I receive the items and their delivery times from Design and Buying, and organise the drops by week.
  • I start creating the products in Shopify.
Week 2

Briefing & shoot

  • I write the briefing with everything I need to launch the drop and share it with Communications so they can do the shoot.
  • The other teams receive it too, with needs such as the size guide or care instructions.
Week 3

E-commerce build

  • I upload the photos to the site.
  • I build the homepage and the New In grid preview.
  • I prepare the content for paid media and newsletters.
  • Pre-launch begins and, if there is one, early access.
Week 4

Drop & analysis

  • Launch on the site, newsletter to the whole list by language, and retargeting and sales campaigns.
  • I update the images in the Klaviyo flows.
  • Segmented follow-up two days later.
  • Post-launch analysis: sales, conversion, email and paid results, and learnings for the next drop.
